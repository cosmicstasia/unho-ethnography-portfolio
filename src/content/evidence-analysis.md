### Research Focus

Our interviews told us how people experience gender in engineering, but we also wanted to see how UT talks about the issue officially. Documents show what the college counts, pays for, and puts on its website. Our conceptual research plan also asked how many women enter and leave engineering, and college reports seemed like the best place to look for that.

### Evidence Collected

I looked at two college reports (the 2023 annual report and the dean's 2026 update to the chancellor), two Women in Engineering program pages, a 2022 conference paper that UT faculty and staff wrote about their programs for women, national faculty data from the Society of Women Engineers, and the page for the engage Engineering Fundamentals program that every first-year engineering student takes.

### Significant Findings

The biggest thing I noticed is that UT reports how many women enroll in engineering but not how many stay. The annual report says women make up 25% of undergraduates and that female enrollment has gone up 212% since 2008. Neither report breaks down retention or faculty by gender, though. That is the exact number our first research question needs, and I couldn't find it anywhere public.

UT's own staff also describe the same problems our participants did. The 2022 paper says women leave partly because of a "lack of mentoring and social support," and women faculty reported implicit bias and feeling isolated. Most of what the college offers in response is about building community: eight women-focused student organizations, a welcome dinner for first-year women, and student ambassadors who help recruit and support women.

Faculty are a different story. Nationally, only about 1 in 5 engineering faculty are women, and aerospace has the lowest share. UT doesn't post its own numbers. Last, the first-year engage program is built around solving problems in teams, so the team conflicts P1 told us about aren't a rare situation. Every first-year student works in groups.

### Connection to the Wicked Problem

These documents made me realize part of the problem is what gets measured. Getting more women into engineering is real progress, and UT is proud of it. But without public retention data by gender, it's hard for anyone to tell whether women are leaving at higher rates, which makes the problem easy to miss. UT's staff know support is an issue and have built programs for it, but none of these documents show whether the programs actually keep women in engineering.

The numbers also helped me picture the classrooms our participants described. If one of P1's 128-person lectures matched the college's 25% average, it would have about 32 women and 96 men. That makes it easier to understand why women tend to sit together.

### Connections to Interviews

A lot of the documents back up what we heard. The 2022 paper's point about mentoring and social support lines up with what our participants valued most. P1 credited a mentor and other women in her program, and P3 and P4 both talked about women sticking together. The student organizations are basically an official version of what students already do at their tables. The 212% growth also matches P3 and P4 saying they've seen more women in engineering over time.

Some of it complicates our interviews. P2 and P3 said almost all of their current engineering professors are women, but nationally women are only about 1 in 5 engineering faculty, and aerospace (both of their majors) has the fewest. Their first year might be the most female faculty they ever have. P4 has had more male professors and noticed the gap herself. This might explain why the first-years we talked to didn't think professor gender mattered much.

The documents also leave things out. The college reports make it look like things are steadily getting better, but P4 is thinking about leaving engineering partly because of gender, and enrollment numbers can't show experiences like hers. Interviewing upperclassmen and finding retention data by gender would help us figure out whether her experience is common.

One limit of this evidence is that the college wrote most of these documents to promote itself, so they focus on good news. The faculty data is also national, not specific to UT.
